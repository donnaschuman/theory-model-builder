Teaching social theory and model building with the Model Builder

What the app does

Students new to theory often write a hypothesis first and then look for a theory to support it. In this app, students start with the theory. They name a population and an outcome, choose a theory, enter its propositions from the seminal source with page numbers, and then draw arrows. Every arrow must say which proposition it comes from, what the reason rests on (the theory, prior research, or practice experience), and why one concept changes the other.

The app does not write content. Students type every proposition, definition, and reason themselves. The app assembles their decisions into a research question, numbered hypotheses or propositions, a path figure, and paper notes, and it runs a self-check that flags gaps: arrows without reasons, concepts without definitions, propositions without page numbers, and arrows whose direction disagrees with the proposition they cite.

Before the first class

  • Directions on every screen. Each step opens with a numbered "What to do" box, and each field has a one-line direction under its label. The wording uses short sentences and common words for students who read English as a second language.
  • No accounts. Students open the app in a current browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or Safari). Nothing to install.
  • Saving. Work saves automatically in the browser on that device. To move between computers or to submit, students press Save my work, which downloads a .json file, and Open saved work to load it again. Tell students to save a file at the end of every session; clearing browser data erases the automatic copy.
  • Look at the finished example first. See a finished example on the home page opens a completed model (treatment engagement among post-9/11 veterans using the Theory of Planned Behavior) on the results page, with every step open for browsing. If a student already has work in progress, the app sets it aside and offers Back to my model.
  • Preview every screen. Add ?preview to the address (social-theory-model-builder.netlify.app/?preview) to move through every step without answering the questions. Next always advances and every step in the list opens. It stays on for that browser tab until you press Exit preview.
  • Decide what students submit. The saved .json file lets you open their full model in the app. The Word notes file and the PNG are what they use in the manuscript.

A teaching sequence

Seven sessions that track the app's steps. Each can be a 30- to 60-minute in-class workshop or a homework module. Adjust the order to your syllabus.

1. Orientation: read a finished model

Project the finished example. Ask students to pick one arrow, find its proposition and page number, read its reason, and decide whether the reason actually explains the mechanism. Then open the Self-check step and discuss why each arrow needs a reason.

Homework: start a model; complete Who, Outcome, and Define outcome.

2. Choosing a theory and finding the seminal source

Students identify the work where the theorist first stated the theory, using what recent papers cite and the "Cited by" count in Google Scholar to confirm it. The theory library lists 112 common theories, models, and frameworks with the seminal source, a note when the theory was substantially revised and by whom, paraphrased common propositions, and key concepts. The app suggests the library citation as a starting point and labels it for students to verify. Students still find each proposition's exact wording and page number in the source.

Before students choose, teach the difference between a topic (what you study, such as veteran suicide risk) and a mechanism (how or why it happens, such as isolation thwarting belongingness, which raises suicidal desire). Theories that name mechanisms supply the arrows.

When a theory was revised (for example, the theory of planned behavior restated as the reasoned action approach by Fishbein and Ajzen in 2010), ask students which version their model uses and to cite that version.

Activity: pairs swap seminal sources and check each other's APA citation against the publisher's record.

3. Extracting propositions

Students find this step the hardest. Demonstrate one proposition in class: quote or paraphrase the theorist, record the page, restate it in plain words, then name the "from" and "to" constructs and the direction. Each student enters two to five propositions per theory.

Check: a partner opens the source to the cited page and confirms the proposition is there. A proposition with no page number shows up on the student's self-check.

4. Building the model

Students add causes (constructs from the propositions fill in automatically, and theory chips can be dragged into the list), decide whether there is an in-between step (mediation), and choose moderators. The app prompts them to consider social determinants both as causes and as moderators, which opens a discussion of person-in-environment and structural explanations.

Two teaching boxes support the course goal of understanding simple mediation and moderation. The In-between step opens with Mediator: how to decide: the why question (Jaccard & Jacoby, 2020), a frustration–aggression diagram, and how a mediator differs from a moderator. The Moderation step opens with Cause or moderator? How to decide: a test for each role, a diagram, and the point that moderators include conditions in the environment, not only personal characteristics (Baron & Kenny, 1986). Its example is social support, which can be a cause of mental health or a moderator of the stress–health link (Cohen & Wills, 1985). The self-check flags a concept used as both a cause and a moderator, and a second theory with no concepts in the model.

After naming the mediator, students decide complete or partial mediation for each cause (Jaccard & Jacoby, 2020, Step 3a). A cause checked for partial mediation gets a curved direct arrow to the outcome, and the mediation hypothesis says which links are completely and which are partially mediated. The finished example uses partial mediation for perceived behavioral control, which the Theory of Planned Behavior holds also predicts behavior directly (Ajzen, 1991).

5. Defining concepts

Students write a conceptual definition and a source for every box and tag the theory each concept comes from. The app shows the outcome's operational definition ("what counts") beside the field for its conceptual definition so students can compare the two.

Activity: collect three definitions anonymously and ask the class which ones describe measurement instead of meaning.

6. Justifying the arrows

For each arrow, students set the direction (+ or −) and line style, link the proposition it comes from (choose it from the menu in the arrow’s panel, or click a proposition and then Link here; dragging also works). Each arrow’s panel starts with a label such as Arrow H1., say what the reason rests on, and write one sentence on how one concept changes the other. Linking a proposition never sets the arrow's direction; students decide it, and the app flags any arrow that disagrees with its proposition. Students who cannot write a reason use "I'm stuck," which walks them through checking their boxes, saying the relationship aloud, or flagging the arrow as needing a source, which becomes a research plan.

7. Results, paper notes, and peer review

Students complete the optional Paper notes step (target journal, problem definitions, each theory's assumptions and history, links to practice), run the Self-check, and download the figure, caption, concepts table, and Word notes. For peer review, students exchange .json files; the reviewer opens the file in the app and comments on arrows that lack reasons or rest on weak propositions.

Reviewing student work

  1. Open the app and choose Open saved work, then select the student's .json file. (This replaces the model in your browser; save your own first if you have one.)
  2. Go to Self-check for a summary of gaps, then Results.
  3. On the figure, click any arrow. The "Change an arrow" panel shows its direction, line style, linked proposition, and reason.
  4. Use the step list on the left to jump to any step the student completed.

Discussion prompts

  • Which of your arrows rests on the theory, and which rests on your practice experience? What source would you need to support a practice-based arrow with theory?
  • Your outcome's operational definition is how you would count it. Does your conceptual definition say what the theory means by it?
  • Where does a social determinant belong in your model: as a cause, or as a moderator of another path? What does each choice imply about where change happens?
  • Which arrow is the weakest claim in your model? What evidence would change your mind about it?
  • What does your theory make visible about the problem, and what does it leave out? Does your model have the same limits?
  • If you used two theories: which concepts appear in both, and do the theories agree on the direction of their effects?

Accessibility and privacy

  • Every drag action has a keyboard alternative: move buttons for reordering causes, menus for linking propositions, and arrow keys for moving figure boxes.
  • Automated checks with axe-core against WCAG 2.2 AA report no violations on any screen of version 1.2.1. Automated checks do not replace testing with assistive technology users.
  • Body text uses Atkinson Hyperlegible Next, a typeface designed by the Braille Institute for low-vision readers.
  • The app runs entirely in the browser. Student work is never sent to a server, and the app collects no student data. If the instructor turns on anonymous visit counts (GoatCounter), only page visits are counted, with no cookies and none of the student's work.

Customizing the app

The app is open source under the MIT license (github.com/donnaschuman/theory-model-builder). To adapt it for your course, copy the repository and edit the settings at the top of index.html:

  • VIDEOS: links to short how-to videos for the Key propositions, In-between, Define concepts, and Why this arrow? steps. YouTube and Vimeo links play inside the step; other links open in a new tab. Empty links hide the slot.
  • SURVEY_URL and ADOPTION_URL: an optional feedback survey and an instructor adoption form.
  • GOATCOUNTER_CODE: optional cookieless visit counts.
  • To add or correct a theory in the library, edit theories.js: group, name, one-sentence description, key concepts, seminal source, revisions (each tagged Revised, Extended, Restated, Challenged, or similar), origins, and paraphrased propositions. Text between asterisks is set in italics. The app and the library page both read this file.

The site has no build step; any static host (Netlify, GitHub Pages, a university web server) can serve the folder.

AI credit, disclosure, and academic integrity

How the app was made

D. L. Schuman designed the Social Theory Model Builder, its teaching approach, and its coaching content, and directed its development. The code, interface text, theory library entries, automated tests, and this guide were drafted and revised with generative AI assistants: Grok (xAI) and Claude (Anthropic; Claude Opus 5.5). The author reviewed and tested the result and is responsible for its content and any errors.

What the app does and does not do

  • The app contains no AI and calls no AI service. Nothing a student types is sent anywhere.
  • It does not generate propositions, definitions, reasons, or hypotheses. Hypothesis and research-question wording is assembled from the student's own entries using fixed sentence templates.
  • "Use a starter" and "I'm stuck" insert fixed fill-in-the-blank sentences, not generated text.
  • The theory library is fixed text. Its references were checked against CrossRef, PubMed, publisher pages, and library catalogs, and its propositions are paraphrases that students must locate in the source.

Suggested syllabus language

Students may use the Social Theory Model Builder to plan their conceptual model. The app does not use artificial intelligence and does not write content; the propositions, definitions, reasons, and analysis in your model must be your own. If you use any generative AI tool for this assignment, follow the course AI policy and disclose that use.

Studying its effect on learning

If you plan to report outcomes from your course (for example, pre and post rubric scores on proposition extraction or model quality, or usability ratings), obtain IRB approval or an exemption determination before you collect data. Saved .json files record each student's decisions in a structured form that can be scored, with the student's consent.
